## Situation before the trade (point-in-time)
**2002 initiation:** Berkshire already had extensive US-dollar assets. Buffett's later explanation concerned structural trade imbalances, not a confidently timed exchange-rate target. Exact initial currencies, rates and start month remain unknown. [1]

## Buffett's thesis in his own words
He felt more comfortable with contracts “at least a partial offset” to Berkshire's dollar position. The 2004 letter says trade policies would put “unremitting pressure on the dollar.” **Later explanations, not 2002 public statements.** [1–2]

## What Berkshire Hathaway did
Started currency positions in 2002, enlarged to $12 billion of open contracts at 2003 year-end and approximately $21.4 billion at 2004 year-end. These are contract amounts, not capital invested or worst-case loss. [1–2]

## What happened after
Reduced direct exposure in 2005 and shifted emphasis toward foreign businesses/international earnings. By 2006 direct exposure was nearly eliminated; that year realized about $186 million pretax profit, not cumulative strategy profit. [3–4]

## Reasoning drivers checklist
- **Imbalance:** Structural external deficit concern. [1–2]
- **Portfolio:** Partial offset, not a freestanding leveraged currency fund. [1]
- **Evolution:** Productive assets became a preferred expression. [3–4]

## Why this trade fits / breaks his pattern
Research interpretation: a genuine macro exception that evolved back toward business ownership. [1–4]

## Public discussion
Independent contemporary rebuttals and original 2003 Fortune trade-deficit essay remain research gaps. [1–2]
