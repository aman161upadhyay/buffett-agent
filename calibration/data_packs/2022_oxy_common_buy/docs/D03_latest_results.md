Technical context: petroleum accounting profits depend on realized prices, volumes, royalties, taxes and the treatment of exploration and development costs. A reserve is not cash in the bank: it needs extraction, transport and often replacement investment. Distinguish upstream earnings from refining and chemical margins rather than assuming they move together. For owner earnings, separate maintenance needed to sustain economic production from expansion capital. Check whether reported free cash flow deducts preferred distributions and whether acquisitions are being called capital expenditure. Proved reserves, production and reserve life use different units and assumptions. A conservative analysis should explicitly test a lower realized-price case without claiming to forecast the commodity. Results discipline: establish which period the release covers and which publication was actually available by the information boundary. Compare growth in units, prices, margins and cash separately, since a higher EPS figure can come from a smaller share count or an accounting item rather than stronger demand. Treat management targets as claims to test against demonstrated history, not as achieved earnings. A weak period may be cyclical, structural or exceptional; describe the evidence needed to distinguish these possibilities. Do not introduce a subsequent quarterly report to explain an ambiguous current observation, and do not confuse disclosure day with the date cash was earned.

Evidence boundary: current production, reported earnings and management's outlook are dated observations, not a forecast of commodity prices or any investor's decision.
