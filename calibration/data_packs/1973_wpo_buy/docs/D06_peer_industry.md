## Numeric competitive economics

The March 17, 1973 annual report gives these FY1972 segment results (USD millions):

| Segment | Revenue | Operating income | Calculated margin |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Newspapers | 99.796 | 10.222 | 10.24% |
| Magazines/books | 93.790 | 5.660 | 6.03% |
| Broadcasting | 24.258 | 5.924 | 24.42% |

The local newspaper carried nearly 79 million advertising lines, up 6.5 million, and almost 63% of metropolitan advertising linage, versus less than 60% in 1971. Daily circulation was approximately 516,000, up 6,000; Sunday circulation just over 686,000, up 15,000 and more than twice the remaining afternoon competitor's Sunday circulation. These are operating-share measures, not national newspaper market share. Broadcasting generated 27% of operating income from 11% of revenue. The $30m newspaper capital-improvement program indicates substantial fixed-asset investment despite apparently attractive franchise economics.

These are contemporaneous operating comparators, not a listed-peer valuation panel. The annual report, 1971 corporate report (https://www.ghco.com/static-files/20476aba-bd06-4a64-91a6-db13e21b7137), and Lu chapter 8 (https://books.google.com/books?id=Lnu0CwAAQBAJ) did not yield matching dated multiples for three public peers. No subsequent competitor exit or price recovery is assumed.

## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

The annual report describes a two-daily Washington market after the Daily News ceases publication, leaving the Post and the Star-News. The Post's Sunday circulation is more than twice the competitor's reported level. Broadcasting earns $5.924 million operating income on $24.258 million revenue, an approximately 24.4% margin, while newspaper publishing earns about 10.2% on revenue, calculated from the report. These are different economic engines and should not share one undifferentiated valuation. A proposed Hartford television-station purchase from Travelers remains subject to approval and other conditions. A private station price would need adjustment for license risk, liabilities and control rights before comparison with minority public shares.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: newspapers and broadcasters sell audiences to advertisers while supplying news and entertainment to readers or viewers. An established local position can be difficult to replicate because a new entrant must build both audience and advertising support. Broadcast licenses add regulatory obligations and are not equivalent to perpetual unregulated monopolies. Advertising is sensitive to business conditions; labor, programming and newsprint costs need not decline when advertising weakens. The distinction between a valuable local franchise and the price paid for it is essential. Scale across properties can help administration, but no unverified synergy is counted in the economics presented here. This paragraph describes the competitive mechanism rather than assigning a private-market valuation to any individual property.

Comparison discipline: differences in accounting, capital intensity and financial leverage can make two nominally similar earnings multiples incomparable. A high return on common equity can come from a strong operating franchise or merely from a thin equity base. The comparison that matters is the durable cash return on all necessary capital, followed by the division of that return among the different claimants.

An outside shareholder cannot assume control benefits or private acquisition synergies. A private buyer's price is useful evidence only if the assets, liabilities and ownership rights are comparable. The alternatives also include retaining liquid funds rather than purchasing a mediocre security merely because it is cheaper than an expensive peer. No industry-average multiple or precise competitor ranking is invented here. The qualitative comparisons identify which operating variables need examination; the absence of a recovered comparable-company table is a genuine limit on numerical relative valuation.
