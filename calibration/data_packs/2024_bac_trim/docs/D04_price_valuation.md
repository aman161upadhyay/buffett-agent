Approximate peers use period-end common shares, book capital and trailing reported EPS, including unusual items; normalized multiples can differ considerably. A low original cost means a realization creates tax. Bank ownership thresholds and reporting requirements are not a rule forcing sale at a particular price. [Source above.]

Technical context: a financial institution's earning assets are supported by deposits, wholesale funding, creditors and equity with different seniority. Regulatory capital cannot all be distributed to investors. Analyze credit quality, liquidity and capital separately: a solvent institution can still face a funding run, and liquid assets do not prove all credit losses are recognized. For a preferred claim, identify whether dividends are cumulative, the issuer's redemption rights and the creditor cushion ahead of the security. For common, normalize provisioning and assess return on tangible equity rather than using industrial free-cash-flow conventions. Reported book value, tangible common capital and market capitalization are different denominators. [Source above; analytical framework rather than additional issuer measurements.]

Valuation discipline: use a consistent claim and denominator. Enterprise value should be compared with cash generation before financing; common value should be compared with cash available after senior claims. Explicitly distinguish sustainable earnings from annualizing one quarter. A current quote from a different date, an eventual transaction price and an investor's later disclosed average cost are not substitutes for the cutoff price. State a range of operating assumptions, compare the result with liquid alternatives and require a margin of safety that survives a less favorable case. When a current input is unavailable, leave the numerical valuation incomplete instead of embedding an untraceable estimate.

Evidence boundary: historical price and book multiples are supplied, while normalized earnings remain a judgment. A reporting threshold is not an automatic valuation rule or a requirement to transact at a specified price.
