## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

The March 17 annual report gives 1972 revenue of $217.844 million, versus $192.749 million in 1971. Newspaper publishing supplies $99.796 million, magazine/book publishing $93.790 million and broadcasting $24.258 million. Operating income is $21.806 million: newspapers $10.222 million, magazine/books $5.660 million, broadcasting $5.924 million. Income before extraordinary items is $10.015 million or $2.08 per share; net income after a $283,000 extraordinary charge is $9.732 million or $2.02. Net quick assets are $29.4 million. The company spent $7.6 million during 1972 on its newspaper plant/office project. Maintenance versus growth capital is not fully separated, so net profit is not simply labeled owner earnings.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: newspapers and broadcasters sell audiences to advertisers while supplying news and entertainment to readers or viewers. An established local position can be difficult to replicate because a new entrant must build both audience and advertising support. Broadcast licenses add regulatory obligations and are not equivalent to perpetual unregulated monopolies. Advertising is sensitive to business conditions; labor, programming and newsprint costs need not decline when advertising weakens. The distinction between a valuable local franchise and the price paid for it is essential. Scale across properties can help administration, but no unverified synergy is counted in the economics presented here. This paragraph describes the competitive mechanism rather than assigning a private-market valuation to any individual property.

Measurement notes: amounts in this reconstruction are nominal dollars, not inflation-adjusted dollars. A historical balance-sheet observation is not a current market quotation. Reported profit, distributable cash and return on tangible operating capital are different quantities. Where a component is missing, owner earnings cannot be calculated precisely by relabeling net income as free cash flow.

The useful reconciliation begins with recurring earnings, adds genuinely noncash charges, and subtracts the expenditures and working capital needed to maintain competitive position. Interest and taxes must be treated consistently with the security being valued. A pretax business yield must not be compared directly with an after-tax common-stock yield. Minority interests, debt, preference claims and cash needed for operations can all change what belongs to the common shareholder. Missing line items are not set to zero. No complete balance sheet, earnings bridge or leverage ratio is implied by the selected figures reproduced here.
