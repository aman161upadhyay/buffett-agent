## Situation before the trade (point-in-time)
**1995 agreement:** Berkshire already owned roughly half GEICO through earlier purchases and GEICO repurchases. Buying the remainder was not a first exposure to an unfamiliar insurer. The January 1996 legal close must not be moved into 1995. [1–2]

## Buffett's thesis in his own words
The business remained exceptional for reasons prevailing in 1951: “rock-bottom operating costs.” **Following-year explanation:** cost structure supports lower prices and customer growth, rather than dependence on forecasting securities markets. [1]

## What Berkshire Hathaway did
Agreed in 1995 to acquire the outstanding interest for $2.3 billion; GEICO became wholly owned January 2, 1996. Berkshire's 1996 letter explains the consequent accounting restatement. [1–2]

## What happened after
The 1996 letter describes strong operating performance and benefits of full ownership. No standalone acquisition IRR is derived from consolidated insurance earnings or float. [2]

## Reasoning drivers checklist
- **Cost moat:** A structural operating advantage, not just cheap insurance shares. [1]
- **Distribution:** Lower selling costs support pricing competitiveness. [1]
- **Familiarity:** A long-observed franchise moved from minority to full ownership. [1–2]

## Why this trade fits / breaks his pattern
Research interpretation: durable advantage plus insurance float, with complete ownership when price and availability permit. It is not a leveraged financial-engineering acquisition. [1–2]
