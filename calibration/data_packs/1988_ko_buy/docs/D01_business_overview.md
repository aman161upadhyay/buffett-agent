Title: The Coca-Cola Company — Business economics
Source: Coca-Cola pre-cutoff annual operating record; management actions reconstructed from Berkshire acquisition-history narrative, with later opinion and purchase disclosures withheld; fund reference https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1986.html
Date: 1988-09-30

## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

Coca-Cola sells branded beverage concentrates through a worldwide bottling and distribution system. Repeat consumer demand and recognition of the flagship product are the proposed economic advantages; concentrate economics differ from the capital required to run a bottling plant. Roberto Goizueta became chief executive in 1981 and works with Don Keough. Their emphasis on the beverage franchise and financial discipline is part of the pre-cutoff operating record. Overseas markets offer different levels of established consumption, creating a possible volume runway without assuming every country will reach the same consumption level. Familiarity with the product is evidence to investigate, not a valuation in itself.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: a concentrate producer sells a small part of the finished beverage's physical volume but supplies a large part of its consumer identity. Bottlers and retailers supply production, packaging and access to the customer. The division of capital and margin among these participants matters when judging returns. A strong brand can support repeat demand, but advertising, product quality and route-to-market access still require resources. Growth abroad may use a local partner's capital, yet the producer remains exposed to execution, currency and regulation. These are implications of the concentrate-and-bottler model, not an assertion that every incremental dollar of revenue becomes owner cash or that consumption can grow without limit.

Economic interpretation, not a management forecast: the important distinction is between demand for the product and cash available to its owners. A recognizable name is not sufficient evidence of an economic franchise. The relevant tests are repeat purchasing, the customer's alternatives, the seller's ability to maintain price, and the capital needed to preserve those advantages. Physical assets can be copied; a durable customer relationship may be harder to reproduce. Conversely, an apparently entrenched relationship can disappear if customers obtain better service or lower prices elsewhere.

The business description therefore does not assume that historical profitability persists indefinitely. Separate ordinary operations from unusual charges and separate a good product from a good investment at the offered price. Management's operating record and the financing of the enterprise belong in the same assessment. No value is assigned here to an unannounced acquisition, hypothetical takeover premium, or an improvement that requires a new owner to change the basic business.
