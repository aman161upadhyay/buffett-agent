## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

For the fiscal year ended June 30, 1998, Microsoft reports $14.484 billion revenue, $4.490 billion net income and $1.67 diluted EPS on the annual report's share basis. Cash and short-term investments total $13.927 billion. Revenue is up from $11.358 billion and net income from $3.454 billion in fiscal 1997. The reported net margin is about 31.0%, calculated as 4.490/14.484. A $296 million acquired in-process technology writeoff related to WebTV affects the reported result; the company's adjusted EPS description must not be substituted silently for GAAP profit. Share splits require consistent units when comparing EPS and the current market quote.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: software involves substantial development and selling expense but little physical manufacturing cost for another copy. Compatibility, application availability and user familiarity can support a platform. Those advantages are not guarantees: a change in computing architecture or distribution can alter which platform controls demand. Reported margins must also be considered alongside stock compensation and the cost of sustaining a development organization. Customer adoption is evidence of current usefulness, not a complete forecast of profits a decade away. Legal limits on business practices can affect the means by which a platform defends its position. A business can be excellent for customers and employees while its shares still require a careful estimate of future cash flows and purchase price.

Measurement notes: amounts in this reconstruction are nominal dollars, not inflation-adjusted dollars. A historical balance-sheet observation is not a current market quotation. Reported profit, distributable cash and return on tangible operating capital are different quantities. Where a component is missing, owner earnings cannot be calculated precisely by relabeling net income as free cash flow.

The useful reconciliation begins with recurring earnings, adds genuinely noncash charges, and subtracts the expenditures and working capital needed to maintain competitive position. Interest and taxes must be treated consistently with the security being valued. A pretax business yield must not be compared directly with an after-tax common-stock yield. Minority interests, debt, preference claims and cash needed for operations can all change what belongs to the common shareholder. Missing line items are not set to zero. No complete balance sheet, earnings bridge or leverage ratio is implied by the selected figures reproduced here.
