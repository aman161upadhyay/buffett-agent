Margins above are operating income divided by total revenue, not the railroads' differently defined operating ratios. FY2008 predates the recession-driven traffic decline: BNSF's Q3 2009 carloads were 2.137 million against 2.590 million, down 17.5% (October 22 release). Do not extrapolate peak-year industry profits unchanged.

Using the same quarter's freight revenue, revenue per carload is approximately $1,633 versus $1,842, down 11.3%. This arithmetic contains fuel surcharges and mix, not pure realized pricing; volume and revenue declines therefore cannot be assigned wholly to a loss of pricing power. D04 compares three dated peer historical earnings multiples with target trailing P/E, and marks that denominator mismatch rather than claiming a common forward screen.

Union Pacific is the closest competing western network; eastern railroads and truck transport provide additional comparisons. Fuel efficiency and network density can favor rail on long hauls, while trucks offer flexibility and door-to-door delivery. A private appraisal should compare capital expenditure, service quality, operating ratio, rate regulation and traffic composition, rather than apply a consumer-franchise multiple to peak railroad earnings. [Source above.]

Technical context: track and terminal networks are costly to replicate but also costly to sustain. Depreciation is an accounting allocation, not a reliable measure of current maintenance investment when equipment prices and traffic requirements change. Separate maintenance and capacity expansion without assuming every reported dollar is optional. Earnings depend on traffic density, mix, fuel, labor and pricing; a network moat does not guarantee stable quarterly volume. Debt belongs in enterprise value and interest belongs in the bridge to equity earnings. A controlling investor's own stock is consideration with economic cost, not free acquisition currency. [Source above; analytical framework, not additional measured network statistics.]

Comparison discipline: compare businesses on the source of their cash and the capital required to earn it, rather than treating the nearest ticker as an exact substitute. Normalize financing and accounting differences before interpreting relative multiples. A peer can have a better balance sheet but a worse franchise, or a better franchise at an unattractive price. Relative cheapness alone cannot prove absolute value. Consider the opportunity cost of adding to an existing understandable holding, retaining liquidity, owning an entire business or buying the investor's own shares. Where a numerical peer table was not recovered, keep the comparison qualitative rather than inventing an industry average.

Evidence boundary: missing figures are not available in contemporaneous sources recovered for this digest. Analytical questions are not additional reported facts or claims about the investor's private thinking. Read the financial, risk and fund-context documents together; no individual accounting ratio supplies a complete investment conclusion.
