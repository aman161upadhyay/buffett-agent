## Situation before the trade (point-in-time)
**1988 major accumulation:** Freddie Mac bought mortgage loans, transformed them into guaranteed mortgage-backed securities, and sold them, earning fees/spreads while avoiding most interest-rate-change risk. The contemporaneous Wesco report explains concern about government control, credit standards and possible future interest-rate bets. Precise initial-purchase timing is unknown: the report says Mutual **increased** its position in 1988, so it does not prove there was no earlier stake. [2]

Year-end 1988 EPS of $5.73 and 27.5% return on average equity are **full-year results reported afterward**, not figures safely available before the initial 1988 purchase. [2]

## Buffett's thesis in his own words
“We expect to hold these securities for a long time.” **Buffett, February 28, 1989**, discussing Freddie Mac and Coca-Cola. [1]

“This is a much better business than that carried on by most (or indeed most of the top 10% of) savings and loan associations, as demonstrated by Freddie Mac's remarkable percentage returns earned on equity capital in recent years.” **Munger**, Wesco 1988 report. This is not a Buffett quotation. Munger supplies the detailed rationale and Buffett endorsed the holding at parent level; the exact individual execution decision is unverified, so the restricted-schema `decider` is `unknown`, not automatically Buffett. [1–2]

## What Berkshire Hathaway did
Mutual Savings, Wesco's thrift subsidiary, increased to 2.4 million when-issued shares, 4% of total shares and then the legal maximum. Average cost was $29.89, approximately $71.7 million total. Buffett says the stock was carried at cost because it was a non-insurance asset, not because market value equaled cost. Original executions and final sale remain unrecovered. [1–2]

## What happened after
Year-end 1988 market price of $50.50 implied $121.2 million value and $49.5 million unrealized pretax gain; Wesco estimated approximately $29.2 million after tax at then-current rates. These are not realized cash proceeds. **Security correction:** Munger says Freddie Mac's “preferred” stock was in substance equivalent to common, so this is not grouped with Berkshire's negotiated redeemable convertible preferreds. [2]

## Reasoning drivers checklist
- **Business model:** fee/spread economics with limited retained duration risk differentiated it from a conventional thrift. [2]
- **Returns:** unusually high equity returns supported attraction, though the full 1988 observation came after purchase. [2]
- **Discount explanation:** unfamiliarity and fears of federal mismanagement were identified; Munger viewed the adverse scenarios as unlikely, not impossible. [2]
- **Access:** legally permitted thrift ownership explains the vehicle and concentration cap. [1–2]

## Why this trade fits / breaks his pattern
Research interpretation: subsidiary-led high-quality financial ownership under regulatory constraints. Do not assign the detailed thesis solely to Buffett, confuse Freddie with Fannie, or import later GSE failures into the 1988 information set. [1–2]

## Public discussion
Wesco's report explicitly discusses fear that federal officials might harm private owners and separates Freddie Mac from the failed FSLIC. This is contemporary management's interpretation of investor concerns, not an independent sentiment survey. [2]
