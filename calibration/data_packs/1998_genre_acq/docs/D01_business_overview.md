## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

General Re is a major reinsurer providing risk capacity to other insurers in the United States and internationally, including its Cologne Re interest. The business combines underwriting with a large invested-asset pool. Ron Ferguson and the existing management provide the operating organization. A financial-products operation adds activities whose exposures differ from traditional property-casualty reinsurance. The proposed combination is a whole-company acquisition through Berkshire shares, not merely a purchase of its investment portfolio. The business description and proposed terms are reconstructed to the agreement date; no subsequent underwriting or derivatives outcome is treated as already known.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: an insurer receives premiums before all associated losses are known or paid. The investment balance generated by that delay is economically valuable only when the eventual underwriting cost is acceptable. Inadequate prices or underestimated reserves can create reported profits that do not exist economically. A direct seller can avoid agency commissions, but saving distribution costs does not compensate for accepting unsuitable risks at inadequate rates. Regulatory capital and claims-paying ability constrain the amount that can be invested in equities or distributed to owners. Reinsurance adds counterparty exposure and can transfer risk without eliminating uncertainty about its measurement. This is a description of insurance mechanics, not a claim that any reported reserve balance is sufficient.

Economic interpretation, not a management forecast: the important distinction is between demand for the product and cash available to its owners. A recognizable name is not sufficient evidence of an economic franchise. The relevant tests are repeat purchasing, the customer's alternatives, the seller's ability to maintain price, and the capital needed to preserve those advantages. Physical assets can be copied; a durable customer relationship may be harder to reproduce. Conversely, an apparently entrenched relationship can disappear if customers obtain better service or lower prices elsewhere.

The business description therefore does not assume that historical profitability persists indefinitely. Separate ordinary operations from unusual charges and separate a good product from a good investment at the offered price. Management's operating record and the financing of the enterprise belong in the same assessment. No value is assigned here to an unannounced acquisition, hypothetical takeover premium, or an improvement that requires a new owner to change the basic business.
