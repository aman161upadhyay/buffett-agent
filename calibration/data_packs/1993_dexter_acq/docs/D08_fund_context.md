Title: Dexter Shoe — Investor's capital and constraints
Source: UPI, September 30, 1993, proposed transaction terms: https://www.upi.com/Archives/1993/09/30/Berkshire-Hathaway-to-buy-Dexter-Shoe-for-420-million/5986749361600/
Date: 1993-09-30

## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

Berkshire has an existing footwear operating base as well as insurance, consumer and media investments. The contemplated Dexter purchase uses Berkshire equity, not an assumed withdrawal of $420 million from cash. Exact September cash and a comparable portfolio denominator were not recovered. Alfond and Lunder's continuity is part of the seller-level operating proposition, while centralized capital allocation remains a buyer concern. A good experience with prior shoe managers can aid diligence but can also encourage overgeneralization. The desk context does not prescribe a target weight; it identifies the permanent ownership interest that would be surrendered and the need to appraise it honestly.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: footwear requires design, purchasing, manufacturing or sourcing, distribution and inventory across styles and sizes. Unsold inventory can be worth less than recorded cost. A good domestic factory can still be vulnerable if imports deliver an acceptable product at materially lower cost. Brand, quality and delivery speed may support customer loyalty, but each must be tested against the consumer's alternatives. Owning stores can capture retail margin while adding leases, inventory and fixed expenses. Efficient operations are valuable only to the extent that they sustain economic returns after all capital requirements. This is a description of competitive possibilities at the cutoff, not a prediction that domestic production must succeed or fail.

Capital-allocation interpretation: a portfolio allocation must fit the legal entity that supplies the money. An insurance subsidiary's invested assets are not all cash available for an acquisition. Premiums collected before claims are paid provide investment funds but also create obligations. Consolidated equity, publicly traded holdings, cash and insurance float are different denominators.

No target weight follows mechanically from the fund-size reference. Available cash at this exact cutoff, where unrecovered, remains unknown rather than being equated with the reference figure. Existing concentrated holdings create opportunity costs; selling them may incur tax and surrender durable earning power. A commitment involving stock also transfers a continuing fraction of the buyer's future economics. The investor has no obligation to purchase simply to remain fully invested. Conversely, waiting has a cost when a sound business is offered below conservative value. This context describes available choices and constraints without specifying an allocation or revealing a decision.
