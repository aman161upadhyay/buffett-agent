## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

USAir has a history of profitable operations, but the exact latest-quarter income statement and an acquisition-adjusted organic unit-cost series were not recovered. D06 supplies the historical reported unit-cost series with its changing scope identified. The integration of acquired airlines changes the route network, fleet and operating obligations; historical stand-alone results may not describe the combined cost base. No later loss, dividend suspension or labor development appears in this snapshot. The available operating evidence supports examining a seasoned management and established business, while leaving open whether margins can withstand deregulated fare competition. A complete current fuel, labor and fleet-cost bridge is necessary for a precise earnings projection and is not invented from eventual outcomes.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: airlines commit capital to aircraft and infrastructure before knowing the revenue each seat will earn. An empty seat cannot be stored for sale tomorrow. Routes, schedules and customer loyalty can support a network, but fare competition can transfer much of the value to travelers. Labor contracts, fleet obligations and fuel costs can remain burdensome even when fares fall. Acquiring another network adds both opportunities and integration exposure. A preferred dividend is paid by the same operating enterprise and therefore depends on cash generation, financing and senior creditor claims. Historical profits should be examined across competitive conditions rather than treated as a bond-like entitlement. No estimate of future fuel prices or an unverified cost-per-seat comparison is assumed here.

Reporting discipline: a full fiscal year's results are not available merely because part of that year has elapsed. The observations above are restricted to the reporting periods identified, and no later quarter is backfilled. An annualized partial period would be a calculation, not a reported result; no such annualization is used unless expressly identified.

For decision purposes, the key question is whether the recent change alters recurring earning power or only its timing. Volume, realized price, operating expenses and capital requirements should be considered separately. A price increase that merely offsets higher input costs is not necessarily an improvement in economics. Similarly, a recovery in accounting profit is not proof that competitive advantage has returned. Where a current operating statistic has not been recovered, this document makes no assertion about its direction. The evidence permits a conditional assessment of durability, not a precise estimate of an unavailable earnings announcement.
