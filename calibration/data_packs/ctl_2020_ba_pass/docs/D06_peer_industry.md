Title: Industry structure and comparable economics
Source: https://investors.boeing.com/investors/news/press-release-details/2020/Boeing-Reports-Fourth-Quarter-Results/default.aspx
Date: 2020-03-20

Airbus is the closest civilian-aircraft rival, but the two companies differ in program mix, cash advances, defense exposure and currency. Aircraft suppliers face different working-capital and certification risks from the airframe assembler. Berkshire's Precision Castparts ownership supplies industry exposure but does not duplicate an aircraft manufacturer's liabilities. Airline customers' capacity plans matter to both producers. The relevant comparison is cash generation after program costs and customer commitments, not only backlog divided by annual revenue. [S1; analytical comparison]

Boeing is one of the two global large-commercial-aircraft manufacturers, with defense, space and services businesses alongside airframes. Certification, engineering, installed fleets and airline training requirements create formidable entry barriers. A bull sees a strategic industrial franchise and long-lived service opportunities, available during overlapping operational and travel crises. The airplane backlog represents substantial customer demand under normal conditions, although cash comes when aircraft are delivered and customer contracts are performed. [S1; analyst interpretation]

### Dated operating baseline

Full-year revenue was $76.559 billion, down 24% from $101.127 billion. Net loss was $636 million versus $10.460 billion of profit; GAAP EPS was negative $1.12 versus positive $17.85. Operating cash flow was negative $2.446 billion versus positive $15.322 billion. Capital expenditure was $1.834 billion, making company-defined free cash flow negative $4.280 billion versus positive $13.600 billion. Cash and marketable securities totaled $10.0 billion at year-end; consolidated debt was $27.3 billion. Commercial deliveries fell to 380 from 806. Company backlog was $463 billion, including over 5,400 commercial airplanes. The company anticipated approximately $4 billion of abnormal production costs, primarily during the current year, from suspension and gradual resumption of MAX production. Those estimates precede the full demand effect of pandemic restrictions. Fourth-quarter dividends alone consumed $1.2 billion despite negative operating cash flow. [S1]

### Underwriting distinction

Comparable-company analysis is useful only after matching the economic claims. A supplier, a platform, a lender and a brand owner can all report revenue growth while requiring very different reinvestment and balance sheets. Growth funded by customers is different from growth funded by new common equity or acquisition debt. Market-share evidence should therefore be read alongside retention, pricing, capital requirements and the company's ability to retain incremental profit. [Analytical interpretation of S1]

No peer multiple is invented where a same-date, same-definition series is missing. The more useful comparison in that case is structural: who owns the customer, who can raise price, who must invest to preserve competitiveness, and who bears a sudden liquidity demand.
