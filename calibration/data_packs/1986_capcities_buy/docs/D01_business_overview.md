Title: Capital Cities/ABC — Business economics
Source: Capital Cities/ABC transaction announced March 18, 1985; Berkshire 1984 letter subsequent-event appendix, https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1984.html
Date: 1986-01-03

## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

Capital Cities is combining its broadcast and publishing operations with ABC, a national television network and related media properties. Tom Murphy and Dan Burke have a long operating record at Capital Cities; the relevant question is whether their low-cost, owner-oriented approach can improve a much larger media group. A large minority block offers participation without operational control of the network. Network programming and local stations have different economics, so strong local-property returns should not be copied mechanically to ABC. The transaction information is reconstructed from the announced combination and pre-cutoff corporate record; no subsequent integration success is assumed.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: newspapers and broadcasters sell audiences to advertisers while supplying news and entertainment to readers or viewers. An established local position can be difficult to replicate because a new entrant must build both audience and advertising support. Broadcast licenses add regulatory obligations and are not equivalent to perpetual unregulated monopolies. Advertising is sensitive to business conditions; labor, programming and newsprint costs need not decline when advertising weakens. The distinction between a valuable local franchise and the price paid for it is essential. Scale across properties can help administration, but no unverified synergy is counted in the economics presented here. This paragraph describes the competitive mechanism rather than assigning a private-market valuation to any individual property.

Economic interpretation, not a management forecast: the important distinction is between demand for the product and cash available to its owners. A recognizable name is not sufficient evidence of an economic franchise. The relevant tests are repeat purchasing, the customer's alternatives, the seller's ability to maintain price, and the capital needed to preserve those advantages. Physical assets can be copied; a durable customer relationship may be harder to reproduce. Conversely, an apparently entrenched relationship can disappear if customers obtain better service or lower prices elsewhere.

The business description therefore does not assume that historical profitability persists indefinitely. Separate ordinary operations from unusual charges and separate a good product from a good investment at the offered price. Management's operating record and the financing of the enterprise belong in the same assessment. No value is assigned here to an unannounced acquisition, hypothetical takeover premium, or an improvement that requires a new owner to change the basic business.
