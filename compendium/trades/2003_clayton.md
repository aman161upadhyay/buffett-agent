## Situation before the trade (point-in-time)
**2003:** Manufactured housing suffered from poor lending, repossessions and low recoveries. Buffett learned from losing money on distressed Oakwood debt; the Clayton case is not a claim that all industry participants were equally sound. [1]

## Buffett's thesis in his own words
Clayton was “the class act of the manufactured housing industry” and “behaved considerably better than its major competitors.” **February 2004 retrospective.** [1]

## What Berkshire Hathaway did
A student gift of Jim Clayton's autobiography helped prompt discussions; Berkshire bought Clayton in 2003. Exact announcement, closing day and purchase multiple remain gaps in this record. [1]

## What happened after
No lifetime return calculated. Lending quality is central to the original distinction; later criticism of consumer-finance practices requires separate evidence and should not be erased or backdated. [1]

## Reasoning drivers checklist
- **Relative quality:** Distinguish Clayton from failed peers. [1]
- **Credit:** Loan origination and retention incentives matter. [1]
- **Product:** Affordable housing can have real customer utility despite broken distribution/financing. [1]

## Why this trade fits / breaks his pattern
Research interpretation: learning from an underwriting mistake led to a better operator, not blanket rejection of a distressed industry. [1]

## Public discussion
Original merger objections and later consumer-finance investigations not authenticated here. [1]
