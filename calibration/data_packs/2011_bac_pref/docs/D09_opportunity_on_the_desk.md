Title: Bank of America — the situation on the desk: capital, confidence and the form a Berkshire investment could take
Source: Bank of America Q2 2011 10-Q and 8-K (Aug 2011); Reuters and Bloomberg coverage of the share-price fall and capital debate, 1-23 Aug 2011; Berkshire's prior negotiated financings as disclosed in Berkshire annual reports (Goldman Sachs 2008, GE 2008, Swiss Re 2009, Dow 2009) — structure reference only
Date: 2011-08-24

## What is on the table
No offer has been made to or by Berkshire. The question for the desk is whether, and in what form, Berkshire should put capital into Bank of America now. Management has said publicly and repeatedly in August 2011 that the bank does not need to raise common equity and is building capital through asset sales and earnings; the market disagrees — the shares have fallen roughly 50% year to date to about $6.30-7.00, credit-default swaps on the holding company have widened to crisis levels, and commentators have published estimates of a $40-50 billion common-equity "hole" tied to mortgage put-backs, litigation and Countrywide. The bank has ~10.1 billion shares outstanding; tangible book value per share is about $12.65; Tier 1 common is 8.2%.

## The forms a Berkshire investment could take
1. Open-market common at ~$7: a claim on normalised earnings power of roughly $1.50-2.00 per share (management's own frame) at 3.5-4.5x, but with full exposure to dilution if a forced capital raise follows.
2. A negotiated private placement of preferred stock with warrants, of the kind Berkshire has provided to Goldman Sachs and General Electric in 2008 (10% cumulative perpetual preferred plus five-year warrants at the market price) and to Swiss Re and Dow in 2009 (12% and 8.5% instruments). For a bank that insists it needs no capital, the feasible coupon is lower than 2008's 10% — a 6% cumulative preferred in the $5 billion range, redeemable at a 5% premium, with ten-year warrants struck near the current price, would be in line with the bank's funding costs (its preferreds trade at 7-9% yields; its 5-year CDS implies ~3.5-4% over Treasuries) and would be worth far more to the bank in confidence than in capital. Any such instrument would count as Tier 1 capital and would be sized so that Berkshire's cash never falls below its $20 billion floor (cash at 30 June 2011: $47.9 billion).
3. Do nothing.

## What an analyst should weigh
The common is priced for a dilutive raise; the preferred form lets Berkshire be paid for supplying confidence while keeping the equity upside through warrants, and the key judgements are (a) whether the deposit franchise (No. 1 US deposit share, ~$1.04 trillion of deposits) and the fee businesses (Merrill Lynch wealth management, cards) remain intact and earn their cost of capital once the mortgage charges end; (b) whether the mortgage liabilities are bounded (the June 2011 $8.5 billion private-label settlement, the $20 billion of Q2 mortgage charges) or open-ended; (c) whether the CEO's clean-up plan (asset sales, 30,000 job cuts, "Project New BAC") is credible; and (d) what coupon and warrant terms would compensate for the risk the equity analysis cannot bound.
