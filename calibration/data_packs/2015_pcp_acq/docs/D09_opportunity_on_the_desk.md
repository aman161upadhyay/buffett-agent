Title: Precision Castparts — the opportunity on the desk: acquiring the whole company
Source: Precision Castparts FY2015 10-K (filed May 2015) and Q1 FY2016 results (July 2015); Berkshire's 13F filings (public PCP holding since 2012, ~4.2 million shares); meeting between Mark Donegan and Berkshire on 1 July 2015 as later described by both parties; share-price data to 7 August 2015
Date: 2015-08-09

## What is on the table
Berkshire already owns about 3% of Precision Castparts (4.2 million shares, a Todd Combs position first reported in 2012). On 1 July 2015 the company's long-serving chief executive, Mark Donegan, met Berkshire's chairman in Omaha; the discussion has turned to whether Berkshire would acquire the whole company. The stock closed on 7 August at $193.88, down from a 2014 high near $275 after four quarters of weak results in the energy-related and pipe businesses and destocking by aerospace customers. A premium of 20% would imply about $235 per share: an equity value of roughly $32 billion on 137 million shares and an enterprise value of about $37 billion including $4.6 billion of net debt — Berkshire's largest purchase ever, financed from its $66.6 billion of cash with perhaps $10 billion of borrowed money, as the chairman has said publicly he would consider for an "elephant".

## The business and price as proposed
Precision Castparts makes investment castings, forgings and aerospace fasteners (about 70% of sales to aerospace, where it is the sole or dominant source on many engine and airframe programmes, with long-term agreements running to the end of the decade; the balance to power and general industrial). FY2015 (March) sales $10.0 billion, operating income $2.6 billion (26% margin), net income $1.53 billion, EPS $10.66, free cash flow ~$1.9 billion; operating margins have exceeded 20% every year since FY2007 and returns on tangible capital are above 20%. At $235 the whole company would cost about 22x trailing earnings, 17.5x on the pre-destocking run-rate, and ~13x EBITDA (FY2015 EBITDA ~$2.9 billion); the 10-year Treasury yields 2.2%. Donegan, 59, would stay and run the business as a Berkshire subsidiary; he has run it since 2002 and is regarded by customers and competitors as the industry's best operator.

## What an analyst should weigh
Whether aerospace sole-source positions and the Boeing/Airbus backlog (eight years of production) make the earnings as durable as Berkshire requires; whether the energy and pipe weakness is cyclical; whether a 5-6% initial earnings yield (on $235) is adequate against 2.2% bonds for a business that needs little capital and can bolt on acquisitions; what price discipline requires when the counterparty expects a premium; and whether an owner-operator CEO staying for a decade is itself part of the asset being bought.
