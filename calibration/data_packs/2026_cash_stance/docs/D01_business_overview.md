Title: An insurer and operating-business owner with permanent capital
Source: https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2025ltr.pdf ; https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/2025ar/202510-k.pdf
Date: 2026-05-01

Berkshire combines insurance float with wholly owned operating companies and a concentrated marketable-equity portfolio. Float is money held to pay future claims, not equity free of obligations. Insurance underwriting and operating subsidiaries create cash inflows, while catastrophes, regulated investment, litigation and subsidiary commitments can create large outflows. The portfolio decision therefore cannot be reduced to whether the next quarter's stock-market return exceeds a Treasury-bill yield. [1,2]

Greg Abel's February letter describes autonomy, accountability, integrity, financial strength and capital discipline. It prefers productive businesses over Treasury holdings, but only at sensible terms and with the balance sheet protected. This is a portfolio-level assessment rather than a new security recommendation. An investor must distinguish cash genuinely needed for insurance resilience from surplus available to deploy over time, and must not count float and equity as two separate pools of unencumbered net worth. Exact immediate distributable capacity across regulated entities is not supplied by one headline cash number. [1,2]

Evidence ledger from the February 28 letter and annual report: 2025 operating earnings were $44.5 billion versus $47.4 billion in 2024; operating cash flow was $46 billion. Float was approximately $176 billion versus $171 billion a year earlier. The insurance-and-other cash, cash-equivalent and Treasury-bill balance, net of unsettled purchases, was $369.0 billion; broader headline cash definitions differ. The letter says total cash and U.S. Treasury holdings exceed $370 billion. Equity securities were $297.8 billion at year end, including holdings outside the U.S. 13F perimeter. There were no own-share repurchases during 2025. Holding-company/BHFC-type borrowings excluding BNSF and BHE were $45.8 billion. The report emphasizes redundant liquidity and independent financing capacity, not a guaranteed forecast of stock-market direction. [1,2]

Analytical distinction: a large customer base is not itself proof of a moat. The relevant test is whether customers keep paying enough to cover the full cost of serving them, including reinvestment and risk. Product descriptions and reported segment economics below are observations; a prediction that their advantages persist is an underwriting judgment. No terminal growth rate, private management forecast, or guaranteed future market share is supplied. Historical success must be tested against the specific competitive threats, not substituted for that test.
