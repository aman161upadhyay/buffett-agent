## Situation before the trade (point-in-time)
**December 1962 initial investment:** a declining New England textile operation, with assets and mill-closure proceeds offering a potential discount realization. The 2014 retrospective dates the first purchase to December 1962 at $7.50; the partnership's earlier narrative gives $7.60. Do not silently harmonize those into a precise weighted-average cost. **1964–1965 control decision:** the investment changed from buying cheap shares anticipating a tender into taking over the underlying bad business. [1–2]

## Buffett's thesis in his own words
“Buying the stock at that price was like picking up a discarded cigar butt that had one puff remaining in it.” **Hindsight**, 2014 letter. [2]

“I became the dog who caught the car.” Same retrospective, describing acquisition of control rather than an intended superior textile franchise. Buffett calls the anger-driven decision “a monumentally stupid decision.” [2]

## What Berkshire Hathaway did
The partnership bought shares from 1962. Buffett says he agreed to tender at $11.50, then received Stanton's written offer of $11.375 in May 1964 and instead bought more. By April 1965 the partnership had 392,633 shares and took control. In 1967 Berkshire, rather than the partnership directly, bought National Indemnity. Textiles remained until the 1985 shutdown. These are separate capital decisions, not a single long stock trade with a textile-only return. [2–3]

## What happened after
Textile economics remained poor despite management efforts and further investment. In 2014 Buffett argued that putting the excellent insurance business inside the partially owned textile company diverted enormous future value from partnership investors; that counterfactual is not a booked textile cash loss. The `loss` classification flags Buffett's own mistake assessment, not negative total shareholder return for the later conglomerate. [2–3]

## Reasoning drivers checklist
- **Cheap assets:** initial justification was price relative to assets, not durable textile pricing power. [1–2]
- **Repurchase catalyst:** the original route to profit did not require owning the factories indefinitely. [2]
- **Anger/control:** the tender-price dispute explains the departure from that route; this is a failure driver, not a recommended investment principle. [2]

## Why this trade fits / breaks his pattern
Research interpretation: separates a potentially sound cigar-butt purchase from an unsound control escalation. Do not train on subsequent conglomerate success as proof the textile takeover was rational. See the separate 1985 exit file. [2–3]

## Public discussion
No original tender document or independent 1964 press response was recovered. Buffett's two first-purchase price accounts conflict slightly and are both retained. [1–2]
