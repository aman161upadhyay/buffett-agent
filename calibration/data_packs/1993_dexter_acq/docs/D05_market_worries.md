Title: Dexter Shoe — Contemporaneous problem and adverse case
Source: UPI, September 30, 1993, proposed transaction terms: https://www.upi.com/Archives/1993/09/30/Berkshire-Hathaway-to-buy-Dexter-Shoe-for-420-million/5986749361600/
Date: 1993-09-30

## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

The strongest sceptical case is that managerial excellence does not create a moat against lower-cost imports. Domestic plants and a capable workforce may still have a structural cost disadvantage. Fashion, inventory sizes and retailer demand can make working capital less recoverable than book value implies. Paying in stock can magnify an overpayment by permanently surrendering participation in the buyer's other businesses. This is not a forecast that Dexter will fail; it is the economically intelligible downside of buying a manufacturer in an import-exposed industry. Original September dissent and a matched domestic-versus-import cost table were not recovered.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: footwear requires design, purchasing, manufacturing or sourcing, distribution and inventory across styles and sizes. Unsold inventory can be worth less than recorded cost. A good domestic factory can still be vulnerable if imports deliver an acceptable product at materially lower cost. Brand, quality and delivery speed may support customer loyalty, but each must be tested against the consumer's alternatives. Owning stores can capture retail margin while adding leases, inventory and fixed expenses. Efficient operations are valuable only to the extent that they sustain economic returns after all capital requirements. This is a description of competitive possibilities at the cutoff, not a prediction that domestic production must succeed or fail.

Adverse-case interpretation: a falling quotation is not itself evidence that a problem is temporary. The economically important loss is an impairment of future cash generation or a financing obligation that existing owners cannot meet. A patient investor can endure a lower stock price, but patience does not repair a destroyed franchise.

The risks described here should be tested before assuming a return to ordinary margins. A capable manager may improve execution without being able to change the industry's economics. A security senior to common stock can still be junior to creditors and can still suffer interruption of its payments. Likewise, apparent balance-sheet strength may depend on estimates whose errors emerge only slowly. No probability is assigned to a disaster without evidence. These concerns are an analyst's reconstruction of the adverse argument from the identified business conditions, not an invented quotation from a named contemporary critic. Original bearish research, where unavailable, is explicitly not represented as recovered.
