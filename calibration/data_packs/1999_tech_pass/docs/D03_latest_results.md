## Reported quarter comparison

January 19, 1999 announcement, corroborated by Reuters at https://www.wired.com/1999/01/whopping-profits-for-microsoft/ . USD millions except EPS, quarter ended December 31:

| Metric | FY1999 Q2 | FY1998 Q2 | Calculated change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue | 4,940 | 3,590 | +37.6% |
| Net income | 1,980 | 1,130 | +75.2% |
| Diluted EPS, pre-March split basis | .73 | .42 | +73.8% |
| Net margin | 40.1% | 31.5% | +8.6 percentage points |

The contemporaneous analyst mean EPS estimate was $.59, with the highest $.61; the reported $.73 exceeds the mean by 23.7%. That is an earnings surprise, not next-quarter guidance. Management attributes demand partly to strong PC shipments and year-2000 replacement spending, which may pull forward purchasing. Neither a numerical multi-year growth guarantee nor a March-quarter result was available in this January announcement. The share basis matters: dividing a subsequently split-adjusted price by the original $.73 EPS would create a false multiple. Revenue, net margin and the earnings surprise are reported business evidence, not a conclusion that an arbitrary market valuation is justified.

## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

The January 19 announcement for the fiscal quarter ended December 31 reports roughly $4.94 billion revenue and $1.98 billion net income, with diluted EPS of $0.73 on the then-announced share basis. Revenue rises about 38% from the year-earlier quarter. These are current business-strength observations; they do not eliminate competitive or legal uncertainty. The fiscal 1998 annual results remain useful as a longer-period reference. A complete quarter-to-cash-flow bridge, a current stock-compensation-adjusted owner-earnings calculation and split-consistent price series are not recovered here. No fiscal 1999 full-year result is included. The recent growth makes the business a plausible buy candidate, not a weak straw-man control.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: software involves substantial development and selling expense but little physical manufacturing cost for another copy. Compatibility, application availability and user familiarity can support a platform. Those advantages are not guarantees: a change in computing architecture or distribution can alter which platform controls demand. Reported margins must also be considered alongside stock compensation and the cost of sustaining a development organization. Customer adoption is evidence of current usefulness, not a complete forecast of profits a decade away. Legal limits on business practices can affect the means by which a platform defends its position. A business can be excellent for customers and employees while its shares still require a careful estimate of future cash flows and purchase price.

Reporting discipline: a full fiscal year's results are not available merely because part of that year has elapsed. The observations above are restricted to the reporting periods identified, and no later quarter is backfilled. An annualized partial period would be a calculation, not a reported result; no such annualization is used unless expressly identified.

For decision purposes, the key question is whether the recent change alters recurring earning power or only its timing. Volume, realized price, operating expenses and capital requirements should be considered separately. A price increase that merely offsets higher input costs is not necessarily an improvement in economics. Similarly, a recovery in accounting profit is not proof that competitive advantage has returned. Where a current operating statistic has not been recovered, this document makes no assertion about its direction. The evidence permits a conditional assessment of durability, not a precise estimate of an unavailable earnings announcement.
