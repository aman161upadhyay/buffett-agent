## Situation before the trade (point-in-time)
**1993:** Berkshire evaluated Dexter as an unusually well-managed shoe manufacturer. The later disappearance of its advantage must not be imported into the acquisition-date assessment. A full contemporary cost model remains unknown. [1–2]

## Buffett's thesis in his own words
“One of the best-managed companies Charlie and I have seen in our business lifetimes.” **March 1994 explanation after acquisition.** The 2007 letter reverses the original competitive assessment and identifies stock payment as aggravating the error. [1–2]

## What Berkshire Hathaway did
Bought Dexter November 7, 1993 and issued 25,203 Berkshire shares. It did not simply spend cash that could be written off without further consequence. [1]

## What happened after
The 2007 retrospective describes the advantage as illusory and the business as worthless. Berkshire's shares continued representing ownership in valuable businesses; their opportunity cost is distinct from Dexter's accounting loss. No invented annualized return or precise shutdown date is supplied. [2]

## Reasoning drivers checklist
- **Original belief:** Exceptional management supported the purchase. [1]
- **Failed assumption:** Competitive protection did not withstand import economics. [2]
- **Financing error:** Permanent Berkshire equity magnified the damage. [1–2]

## Why this trade fits / breaks his pattern
Research interpretation: managerial excellence cannot rescue structurally unfavorable economics. Paying in stock creates a second valuation decision: what ownership is being surrendered? [2]

## Public discussion
Buffett's later self-criticism is verified; independently dated 1993 dissent and modern forum analyses remain gaps. Do not confuse later criticism with entry-date consensus. [1–2]
