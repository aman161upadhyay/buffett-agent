Title: H.J. Heinz — the proposal on the table: a partnership buyout with 3G Capital
Source: Discussions reported between 3G Capital (Jorge Paulo Lemann) and Berkshire from December 2012, as later described in both parties' public statements; Heinz FY2012 10-K and Q2 FY2013 10-Q for the financial figures; Burger King 2010 buyout and Anheuser-Busch InBev integration record for 3G's operating history (public record to 2012)
Date: 2013-02-13

## What is on the table
3G Capital, the Brazilian investment firm run by Jorge Paulo Lemann, Marcel Telles and Carlos Alberto Sicupira (the operators behind AmBev, InBev's purchase of Anheuser-Busch in 2008 and the 2010 Burger King buyout), has proposed that Berkshire join it in acquiring H.J. Heinz outright. Heinz's board is being approached at a price around $72.50 per share in cash — about a 20% premium to the stock's all-time high of roughly $60.50 and an equity value near $23 billion, $28 billion including Heinz's ~$5 billion of net debt. Berkshire's proposed role is as the capital partner: a total commitment in the region of $12 billion, split between (i) about half of the common equity (roughly $4 billion, matched by 3G's $4 billion) and (ii) about $8 billion of preferred stock with a 9% cumulative dividend, redeemable at a premium, plus warrants to buy ~5% of the common for a nominal sum. 3G would run the company: it would install its own CEO and apply zero-based budgeting, as it did at Burger King where operating margins roughly doubled within two years. The balance of the purchase price (about $7 billion) would be new debt raised at Heinz, which would take leverage to roughly 5x EBITDA at closing.

## The economics as proposed
On FY2012 figures Heinz earned $1.09 billion of EBIT ($1.58 billion EBITDA) on $11.6 billion of sales, with 29 consecutive quarters of organic growth, 60% of sales outside North America and 25% in emerging markets; ketchup holds about 60% of the US market and the Heinz brand is No. 1 or 2 in its categories in most of its markets. At $72.50 the whole company changes hands at about 21x trailing earnings and ~14.5x EBITDA; the common equity that Berkshire would buy sits beneath the new debt and the 9% preferred. The preferred's 9% coupon compares with Heinz's existing bonds at 3-4% and the 10-year Treasury at 2.0%. Berkshire's return on the package would therefore be a 9% contractual yield on two-thirds of its money plus the equity's share of whatever 3G's cost programme and the brands' growth produce, less the leverage risk.

## What an analyst should weigh
Whether the brands are durable enough to carry 5x leverage; whether 3G's cost record transfers to a food company; what the preferred's seniority and redemption terms are worth if the common underperforms; what the deal does to Berkshire's $47 billion of cash and its pipeline of elephant-sized deals; and whether paying a 20% premium to an all-time high can be reconciled with Berkshire's acquisition criteria when two-thirds of the capital is a 9% senior instrument.
