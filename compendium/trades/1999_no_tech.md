## Situation before the trade (point-in-time)
**November 1999:** The Fortune essay contrasted two 17-year market periods and examined interest rates, corporate profits and investor expectations. Its automobile/aviation analogies addressed the difference between societal transformation and shareholder profit. [1–2]

## Buffett's thesis in his own words
“It's hard to come up with a persuasive case” that the next 17 years would resemble the previous 17. The essay's approximately 6% aggregate-return scenario was conditional, not a precise near-term crash forecast. [1–2]

## What Berkshire Hathaway did
Published an expectations/valuation argument and maintained investment discipline. No Nasdaq short, universal rejection of technology products or market-timing liquidation is established by this source. [1–2]

## What happened after
This file deliberately does not use the subsequent technology crash as information available in 1999, or calculate a fictitious abstention IRR. [1–2]

## Reasoning drivers checklist
- **Industry versus security:** Growth can coexist with poor investor results. [1–2]
- **Valuation:** Starting prices and underlying economic returns constrain outcomes. [1–2]
- **Competence:** Uncertainty about winners supports abstention rather than automatic shorting. [1–2]

## Why this trade fits / breaks his pattern
Research interpretation: a pass decision and an expectations framework, not a directional macro trade with a strike price. [1–2]

Fortune/Carol Loomis provided the contemporary publication channel. Independent contemporary rebuttals remain a gap. [1]
