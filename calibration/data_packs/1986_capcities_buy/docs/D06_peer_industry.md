## Quantitative media economics

Capital Cities' 1984 annual report, available in March 1985, gives the following standalone segment comparison:

| FY1984 segment ($m) | Revenue | Operating income before corporate allocation | Margin |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Broadcasting | 271.848 | 141.638 | 52.1% |
| Publishing | 591.616 | 133.179 | 22.5% |
| Cable | 76.258 | 2.544 | 3.3% |

Cable operating cash flow was $25.6m, a 33.6% margin, but depreciation $15.9m and intangible amortization $7.2m sharply reduced accounting income. Basic subscribers grew from 350,000 to 367,000 and premium units from 250,000 to 265,000. These are different units and must not be added as unique households.

The report places continental-US cable penetration at 42% in 1984, against 38% in 1983 and 19% in 1979. It estimates cable advertising of $500m, up 45%, and barter advertising near $500m, up 33%. These figures quantify competitive alternatives to broadcast advertising. The regulatory television ownership ceiling was 25% of national television homes under the changed rules, not unlimited network market power.

## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

Local broadcast franchises and a national network both sell advertising, but their competitive costs and operating risks differ. The network must continually offer programs that attract viewers and affiliates; an established local station may have a more stable position in its market. Publishing properties add another earnings stream and another set of costs. No contemporaneous comparable-company earnings table is recovered. Acquisition prices in a fashionable sector can be high even when individual assets are excellent. A fair comparison needs both the managerial record and the capital committed to the combination, rather than treating all media earnings as identical or assuming a repeat of earlier property-value bargains.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: newspapers and broadcasters sell audiences to advertisers while supplying news and entertainment to readers or viewers. An established local position can be difficult to replicate because a new entrant must build both audience and advertising support. Broadcast licenses add regulatory obligations and are not equivalent to perpetual unregulated monopolies. Advertising is sensitive to business conditions; labor, programming and newsprint costs need not decline when advertising weakens. The distinction between a valuable local franchise and the price paid for it is essential. Scale across properties can help administration, but no unverified synergy is counted in the economics presented here. This paragraph describes the competitive mechanism rather than assigning a private-market valuation to any individual property.

Comparison discipline: differences in accounting, capital intensity and financial leverage can make two nominally similar earnings multiples incomparable. A high return on common equity can come from a strong operating franchise or merely from a thin equity base. The comparison that matters is the durable cash return on all necessary capital, followed by the division of that return among the different claimants.

An outside shareholder cannot assume control benefits or private acquisition synergies. A private buyer's price is useful evidence only if the assets, liabilities and ownership rights are comparable. The alternatives also include retaining liquid funds rather than purchasing a mediocre security merely because it is cheaper than an expensive peer. No industry-average multiple or precise competitor ranking is invented here. The qualitative comparisons identify which operating variables need examination; the absence of a recovered comparable-company table is a genuine limit on numerical relative valuation.
