Acquired-carrier integration changes the 1988 scope, limiting organic comparisons. Load-factor headroom over breakeven narrowed from 8.0 points in 1987 to 2.6 in 1988. Yield increased 2.8%, but unit cost increased 4.9%. These measures quantify competitive and fixed-cost exposure without predicting subsequent losses.

FY1988 fuel consumed was 617m gallons at an average 52.58 cents per gallon; fleet was 226 aircraft and year-end employees 24,337. A hypothetical ten-cent/gallon fuel increase at unchanged volume would add $61.7m pretax cost, 14.2% of Group historical operating income, before pricing/hedging offsets; this is sensitivity analysis, not a forecast.

A three-company valuation panel remains unrecovered after these report tables, the April 28 earnings article and July 18 industry preview. No later failures, rescues or investor assessments are imported as evidence.

## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

The appropriate peer comparison is cost per unit of service, route quality, fleet efficiency, labor flexibility, debt and leases, not merely years of past profitability. Low-fare entrants can influence pricing even when a large incumbent retains substantial traffic. An acquisition can improve network reach while increasing integration and cost exposure. No complete contemporaneous peer unit-cost table has been recovered, a central limitation rather than a reason to assume parity. Relative to a consumer-franchise common stock, this security relies more heavily on coupon, redemption and priority. Relative to a plain bond, it offers conversion but remains exposed to the same operating enterprise and its senior financing claims.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: airlines commit capital to aircraft and infrastructure before knowing the revenue each seat will earn. An empty seat cannot be stored for sale tomorrow. Routes, schedules and customer loyalty can support a network, but fare competition can transfer much of the value to travelers. Labor contracts, fleet obligations and fuel costs can remain burdensome even when fares fall. Acquiring another network adds both opportunities and integration exposure. A preferred dividend is paid by the same operating enterprise and therefore depends on cash generation, financing and senior creditor claims. Historical profits should be examined across competitive conditions rather than treated as a bond-like entitlement. No estimate of future fuel prices or an unverified cost-per-seat comparison is assumed here.

Comparison discipline: differences in accounting, capital intensity and financial leverage can make two nominally similar earnings multiples incomparable. A high return on common equity can come from a strong operating franchise or merely from a thin equity base. The comparison that matters is the durable cash return on all necessary capital, followed by the division of that return among the different claimants.

An outside shareholder cannot assume control benefits or private acquisition synergies. A private buyer's price is useful evidence only if the assets, liabilities and ownership rights are comparable. The alternatives also include retaining liquid funds rather than purchasing a mediocre security merely because it is cheaper than an expensive peer. No industry-average multiple or precise competitor ranking is invented here. The qualitative comparisons identify which operating variables need examination; the absence of a recovered comparable-company table is a genuine limit on numerical relative valuation.
