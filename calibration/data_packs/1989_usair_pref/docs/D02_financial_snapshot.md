## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

The proposed investment is $358 million of 9.25% convertible preferred, providing $33.115 million annual contractual dividends by multiplication. Conversion is at $60 a share after two years, implying about 5.967 million shares before any contractual adjustment if all principal converts. The instrument has ten-year mandatory redemption and dividend-arrearage provisions; the complete original agreement is not recovered. Preferred ranking is senior to common equity but does not place Berkshire ahead of secured lenders and other senior creditors. Historical unit costs and lease commitments are now supplied; a detailed lease-capitalization reconciliation and fleet-capital budget remain unrecovered. The contractual dividend is not the same as a guaranteed economic yield.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: airlines commit capital to aircraft and infrastructure before knowing the revenue each seat will earn. An empty seat cannot be stored for sale tomorrow. Routes, schedules and customer loyalty can support a network, but fare competition can transfer much of the value to travelers. Labor contracts, fleet obligations and fuel costs can remain burdensome even when fares fall. Acquiring another network adds both opportunities and integration exposure. A preferred dividend is paid by the same operating enterprise and therefore depends on cash generation, financing and senior creditor claims. Historical profits should be examined across competitive conditions rather than treated as a bond-like entitlement. No estimate of future fuel prices or an unverified cost-per-seat comparison is assumed here.

Measurement notes: amounts in this reconstruction are nominal dollars, not inflation-adjusted dollars. A historical balance-sheet observation is not a current market quotation. Reported profit, distributable cash and return on tangible operating capital are different quantities. Where a component is missing, owner earnings cannot be calculated precisely by relabeling net income as free cash flow.

The useful reconciliation begins with recurring earnings, adds genuinely noncash charges, and subtracts the expenditures and working capital needed to maintain competitive position. Interest and taxes must be treated consistently with the security being valued. A pretax business yield must not be compared directly with an after-tax common-stock yield. Minority interests, debt, preference claims and cash needed for operations can all change what belongs to the common shareholder. Missing line items are not set to zero. No complete balance sheet, earnings bridge or leverage ratio is implied by the selected figures reproduced here.
