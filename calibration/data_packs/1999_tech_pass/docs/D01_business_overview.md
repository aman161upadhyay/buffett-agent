## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

Microsoft sells widely used personal-computer operating systems and application software. Windows and Office benefit from installed users, compatible applications, developer support and familiarity. The software model has low physical reproduction cost and very high current profitability. Bill Gates leads an organization with extensive technical and distribution capability. A plausible investment case is an entrenched platform whose cash generation can grow with personal computing. The uncertainty is whether current leadership predicts the economics of future architectures and Internet distribution. This document does not quote the investor's own opinion or reveal whether the company is inside his competence; it supplies the business evidence needed to assess that question.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: software involves substantial development and selling expense but little physical manufacturing cost for another copy. Compatibility, application availability and user familiarity can support a platform. Those advantages are not guarantees: a change in computing architecture or distribution can alter which platform controls demand. Reported margins must also be considered alongside stock compensation and the cost of sustaining a development organization. Customer adoption is evidence of current usefulness, not a complete forecast of profits a decade away. Legal limits on business practices can affect the means by which a platform defends its position. A business can be excellent for customers and employees while its shares still require a careful estimate of future cash flows and purchase price.

Economic interpretation, not a management forecast: the important distinction is between demand for the product and cash available to its owners. A recognizable name is not sufficient evidence of an economic franchise. The relevant tests are repeat purchasing, the customer's alternatives, the seller's ability to maintain price, and the capital needed to preserve those advantages. Physical assets can be copied; a durable customer relationship may be harder to reproduce. Conversely, an apparently entrenched relationship can disappear if customers obtain better service or lower prices elsewhere.

The business description therefore does not assume that historical profitability persists indefinitely. Separate ordinary operations from unusual charges and separate a good product from a good investment at the offered price. Management's operating record and the financing of the enterprise belong in the same assessment. No value is assigned here to an unannounced acquisition, hypothetical takeover premium, or an improvement that requires a new owner to change the basic business.
