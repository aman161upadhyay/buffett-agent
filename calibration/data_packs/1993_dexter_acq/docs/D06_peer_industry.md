## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

H. H. Brown's work shoes and boots, Lowell's women's and nurses' shoes, and Dexter's popular-priced men's and women's lines serve different segments. A strong result in one does not establish identical customer loyalty or sourcing economics in another. Import competition must be compared on delivered cost, quality, reliability and inventory speed, not only on factory wage rates. No verified September competitor margins or acquisition-comparison table were recovered. A vertically integrated manufacturer with outlets can capture more of the retail sale but also needs more inventory and fixed expense. The question is durability of returns after all required capital, not merely whether an admired manager runs a tidy factory.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: footwear requires design, purchasing, manufacturing or sourcing, distribution and inventory across styles and sizes. Unsold inventory can be worth less than recorded cost. A good domestic factory can still be vulnerable if imports deliver an acceptable product at materially lower cost. Brand, quality and delivery speed may support customer loyalty, but each must be tested against the consumer's alternatives. Owning stores can capture retail margin while adding leases, inventory and fixed expenses. Efficient operations are valuable only to the extent that they sustain economic returns after all capital requirements. This is a description of competitive possibilities at the cutoff, not a prediction that domestic production must succeed or fail.

Comparison discipline: differences in accounting, capital intensity and financial leverage can make two nominally similar earnings multiples incomparable. A high return on common equity can come from a strong operating franchise or merely from a thin equity base. The comparison that matters is the durable cash return on all necessary capital, followed by the division of that return among the different claimants.

An outside shareholder cannot assume control benefits or private acquisition synergies. A private buyer's price is useful evidence only if the assets, liabilities and ownership rights are comparable. The alternatives also include retaining liquid funds rather than purchasing a mediocre security merely because it is cheaper than an expensive peer. No industry-average multiple or precise competitor ranking is invented here. The qualitative comparisons identify which operating variables need examination; the absence of a recovered comparable-company table is a genuine limit on numerical relative valuation.
