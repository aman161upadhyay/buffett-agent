## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

USAir operates an established passenger-airline network and has expanded through acquisitions including Piedmont and Pacific Southwest. Ed Colodny leads the company and has a substantial industry operating record. A network and customer base are real operating assets, but their economics differ from those of a branded product with little continuing capital need. The investment under consideration is a negotiated convertible preferred, not a forecast that airline common stocks possess uniformly attractive long-term economics. Ownership stability and takeover speculation are part of the financing context. The reconstruction presents the company as a plausible going concern with demonstrated profits, not as an already-known failed investment.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: airlines commit capital to aircraft and infrastructure before knowing the revenue each seat will earn. An empty seat cannot be stored for sale tomorrow. Routes, schedules and customer loyalty can support a network, but fare competition can transfer much of the value to travelers. Labor contracts, fleet obligations and fuel costs can remain burdensome even when fares fall. Acquiring another network adds both opportunities and integration exposure. A preferred dividend is paid by the same operating enterprise and therefore depends on cash generation, financing and senior creditor claims. Historical profits should be examined across competitive conditions rather than treated as a bond-like entitlement. No estimate of future fuel prices or an unverified cost-per-seat comparison is assumed here.

Economic interpretation, not a management forecast: the important distinction is between demand for the product and cash available to its owners. A recognizable name is not sufficient evidence of an economic franchise. The relevant tests are repeat purchasing, the customer's alternatives, the seller's ability to maintain price, and the capital needed to preserve those advantages. Physical assets can be copied; a durable customer relationship may be harder to reproduce. Conversely, an apparently entrenched relationship can disappear if customers obtain better service or lower prices elsewhere.

The business description therefore does not assume that historical profitability persists indefinitely. Separate ordinary operations from unusual charges and separate a good product from a good investment at the offered price. Management's operating record and the financing of the enterprise belong in the same assessment. No value is assigned here to an unannounced acquisition, hypothetical takeover premium, or an improvement that requires a new owner to change the basic business.
