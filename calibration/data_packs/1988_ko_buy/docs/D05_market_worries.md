Title: The Coca-Cola Company — Contemporaneous problem and adverse case
Source: Coca-Cola pre-cutoff annual operating record; management actions reconstructed from Berkshire acquisition-history narrative, with later opinion and purchase disclosures withheld; fund reference https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/1986.html
Date: 1988-09-30

## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

The adverse case is that a powerful brand is already fully valued, international growth disappoints, or financial returns are diluted by capital-heavy bottling investment and unrelated activities. Taste preferences and aggressive competitors can change the economics even when consumers recognize the name. Currency movements can obscure underlying progress. The product is inexpensive per serving, but that alone does not prove unlimited pricing power. The stock's performance after the preceding year's market crash is not a proof of value today. Original September bearish analyst research and precise price sensitivity were not recovered. The concern is overpayment or franchise erosion, not hindsight knowledge of a future health-policy debate.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: a concentrate producer sells a small part of the finished beverage's physical volume but supplies a large part of its consumer identity. Bottlers and retailers supply production, packaging and access to the customer. The division of capital and margin among these participants matters when judging returns. A strong brand can support repeat demand, but advertising, product quality and route-to-market access still require resources. Growth abroad may use a local partner's capital, yet the producer remains exposed to execution, currency and regulation. These are implications of the concentrate-and-bottler model, not an assertion that every incremental dollar of revenue becomes owner cash or that consumption can grow without limit.

Adverse-case interpretation: a falling quotation is not itself evidence that a problem is temporary. The economically important loss is an impairment of future cash generation or a financing obligation that existing owners cannot meet. A patient investor can endure a lower stock price, but patience does not repair a destroyed franchise.

The risks described here should be tested before assuming a return to ordinary margins. A capable manager may improve execution without being able to change the industry's economics. A security senior to common stock can still be junior to creditors and can still suffer interruption of its payments. Likewise, apparent balance-sheet strength may depend on estimates whose errors emerge only slowly. No probability is assigned to a disaster without evidence. These concerns are an analyst's reconstruction of the adverse argument from the identified business conditions, not an invented quotation from a named contemporary critic. Original bearish research, where unavailable, is explicitly not represented as recovered.
