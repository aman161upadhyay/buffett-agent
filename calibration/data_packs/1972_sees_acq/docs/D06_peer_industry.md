## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

The relevant competitors are boxed-chocolate makers, local confectioners and alternative gifts, rather than a commodity-food index alone. Store-level reputation and gifting reliability can support a premium, but chocolate ingredients are purchased in competitive markets. Another manufacturer with similar machinery is not necessarily a comparable franchise. An acquisition at several times tangible capital is attractive only if the cash earnings exceed those of an ordinary producer without demanding equivalent continuing reinvestment. Competitor margins and a dated transaction-comparison table are not available in contemporaneous sources recovered here. The low-capital thesis is grounded in the seller's operating scale, not in a claim that no future spending is necessary.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: boxed chocolates are purchased both for consumption and as gifts. The purchaser's concern with the recipient's reaction can make dependable quality and an established local name important. That does not eliminate substitution by other sweets or gifts. The retailer must carry inventory, staff shops and maintain product freshness even when demand is concentrated in holidays. Profits depend on realized price per pound after ingredients, labor, occupancy and selling costs. A premium brand has value only if the realized premium exceeds the extra cost of sustaining quality. Regional recognition cannot be assumed to transfer automatically to a new city. These are business-model implications, not an assumption of national expansion or a promised price increase.

Comparison discipline: differences in accounting, capital intensity and financial leverage can make two nominally similar earnings multiples incomparable. A high return on common equity can come from a strong operating franchise or merely from a thin equity base. The comparison that matters is the durable cash return on all necessary capital, followed by the division of that return among the different claimants.

An outside shareholder cannot assume control benefits or private acquisition synergies. A private buyer's price is useful evidence only if the assets, liabilities and ownership rights are comparable. The alternatives also include retaining liquid funds rather than purchasing a mediocre security merely because it is cheaper than an expensive peer. No industry-average multiple or precise competitor ranking is invented here. The qualitative comparisons identify which operating variables need examination; the absence of a recovered comparable-company table is a genuine limit on numerical relative valuation.
