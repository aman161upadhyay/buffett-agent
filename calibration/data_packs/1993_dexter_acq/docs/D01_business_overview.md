Title: Dexter Shoe — Business economics
Source: UPI, September 30, 1993, proposed transaction terms: https://www.upi.com/Archives/1993/09/30/Berkshire-Hathaway-to-buy-Dexter-Shoe-for-420-million/5986749361600/
Date: 1993-09-30

## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

Dexter makes popular-priced men's and women's shoes and combines manufacturing with distribution and retail outlets. Harold Alfond and Peter Lunder supply a long operating history. The proposed business is being evaluated as a continuing operation with incumbent management, not as factories to liquidate. Berkshire already understands footwear through H. H. Brown and Lowell Shoe, but familiarity with those operations is not proof that every shoe company has the same competitive strength. The reconstruction portrays Dexter as an efficient established manufacturer. Whether efficiency and customer loyalty protect domestic production against imports remains an open economic question.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: footwear requires design, purchasing, manufacturing or sourcing, distribution and inventory across styles and sizes. Unsold inventory can be worth less than recorded cost. A good domestic factory can still be vulnerable if imports deliver an acceptable product at materially lower cost. Brand, quality and delivery speed may support customer loyalty, but each must be tested against the consumer's alternatives. Owning stores can capture retail margin while adding leases, inventory and fixed expenses. Efficient operations are valuable only to the extent that they sustain economic returns after all capital requirements. This is a description of competitive possibilities at the cutoff, not a prediction that domestic production must succeed or fail.

Economic interpretation, not a management forecast: the important distinction is between demand for the product and cash available to its owners. A recognizable name is not sufficient evidence of an economic franchise. The relevant tests are repeat purchasing, the customer's alternatives, the seller's ability to maintain price, and the capital needed to preserve those advantages. Physical assets can be copied; a durable customer relationship may be harder to reproduce. Conversely, an apparently entrenched relationship can disappear if customers obtain better service or lower prices elsewhere.

The business description therefore does not assume that historical profitability persists indefinitely. Separate ordinary operations from unusual charges and separate a good product from a good investment at the offered price. Management's operating record and the financing of the enterprise belong in the same assessment. No value is assigned here to an unannounced acquisition, hypothetical takeover premium, or an improvement that requires a new owner to change the basic business.
