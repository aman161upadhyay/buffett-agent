## Situation before the trade (point-in-time)
**July 1989:** unlike the airline, paper and investment-banking industries represented in other preferred investments, Buffett believed he could understand Gillette's business economics and make a reasonably intelligent forecast. Original acquisition-date financials and the precise contemporaneous common-equity valuation are unrecovered. Do not use later Sensor results or the eventual P&G transaction as entry evidence. [1]

## Buffett's thesis in his own words
“Gillette's business is very much the kind we like.” **Post-purchase**, March 2, 1990. [1]

“We only want to link up with people whom we like, admire, and trust.” Same letter, expressly including Colman Mockler and the other preferred issuers' CEOs. Gillette was distinguished as the superior, more predictable business, not grouped indiscriminately with them. [1]

## What Berkshire Hathaway did
Bought $600 million preferred in July 1989, 8.75% dividend, mandatory redemption in ten years and conversion at $50/share. Buffett joined Gillette's board. Restrictions prevented exploiting short-term common-price blips. A call forced conversion into 12 million then-current common shares on April 1, 1991. The preferred episode ends at conversion, but the common investment continues separately; the 21-month duration is approximate calendar arithmetic. [1–3]

## What happened after
The common received was worth $2.502 billion at year-end 1995. Yet Buffett called the security selection an opportunity-cost mistake: $600 million might instead have purchased 60 million split-adjusted common shares, versus 48 million through conversion. He estimated $625 million extra value forgone at year-end 1995, less roughly $70 million excess preferred dividends. That alternative placement was explicitly probabilistic, not an executed offer. [3]

## Reasoning drivers checklist
- **Franchise:** business economics were understandable and expected to outperform ordinary industry returns. [1]
- **Manager:** Mockler met the character/ability test. [1]
- **Structure:** income and conversion option supplied the original financing proposition. [1]
- **Ownership:** long-term participation and voting rights, not arbitrage, were intended. [1]

## Why this trade fits / breaks his pattern
Research interpretation: a very profitable investment can still contain a capital-allocation mistake. Excess protection and yield sacrificed common-equity upside in the one preferred issuer Buffett understood especially well. [3]

## Public discussion
Original financing documents and contemporaneous takeover-defense debate remain gaps. Later reports that preferred yield was effectively tax-free for Berkshire must be checked against the insurer dividend-tax changes Buffett explains in 1995. [3]
