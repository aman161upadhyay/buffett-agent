Operating income includes $180m restructuring/disinvestment provisions in 1986 and $36m in 1987. Adding back the latter gives $1,360m, a calculation, not GAAP operating income. Net income includes nonoperating/ownership-transaction effects and, in earlier years, discontinued operations; it is not interchangeable with continuing income.

FY1987 cash plus marketable securities was $1,468m, giving simple net debt $1,234m. Return on average equity from continuing operations was 27.2%; the historical 1986 continuing-income ratio is 28.8%, not the erroneous 23.8% in a secondary transcription. Approximately 372m shares were outstanding at year-end; 377m was the weighted average. The $.90 in-kind dividend is separate from the $1.12 cash dividend.

A maintenance-capex-only owner-earnings bridge remains unverified after the annual report, the July 15, 1988 interim article and Lu chapter 14 (https://books.google.com/books?id=Lnu0CwAAQBAJ). Depreciation is not assumed equal to maintenance capex.

## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

The latest fully completed fiscal-year reference is 1987. Historical income-statement and year-end share data are supplied above; a current diluted share count and maintenance-capital reconciliation remain unrecovered, so a numeric owner-earnings estimate is not asserted. The business model separates brand and concentrate returns from bottler capital requirements; consolidated accounts must still be checked for owned bottling exposure and investments. High potential returns on tangible operating capital do not mean that working capital, marketing or distribution cost nothing. A current leverage ratio and September trailing earnings multiple are not available in contemporaneous sources recovered here. No subsequent annual purchase table is substituted for company financial statements.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: a concentrate producer sells a small part of the finished beverage's physical volume but supplies a large part of its consumer identity. Bottlers and retailers supply production, packaging and access to the customer. The division of capital and margin among these participants matters when judging returns. A strong brand can support repeat demand, but advertising, product quality and route-to-market access still require resources. Growth abroad may use a local partner's capital, yet the producer remains exposed to execution, currency and regulation. These are implications of the concentrate-and-bottler model, not an assertion that every incremental dollar of revenue becomes owner cash or that consumption can grow without limit.

Measurement notes: amounts in this reconstruction are nominal dollars, not inflation-adjusted dollars. A historical balance-sheet observation is not a current market quotation. Reported profit, distributable cash and return on tangible operating capital are different quantities. Where a component is missing, owner earnings cannot be calculated precisely by relabeling net income as free cash flow.

The useful reconciliation begins with recurring earnings, adds genuinely noncash charges, and subtracts the expenditures and working capital needed to maintain competitive position. Interest and taxes must be treated consistently with the security being valued. A pretax business yield must not be compared directly with an after-tax common-stock yield. Minority interests, debt, preference claims and cash needed for operations can all change what belongs to the common shareholder. Missing line items are not set to zero. No complete balance sheet, earnings bridge or leverage ratio is implied by the selected figures reproduced here.
