## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

Acquisition-history reconstructions describe approximately $29–30 million annual sales, roughly $4–4.2 million pretax earnings, approximately $2 million after-tax earnings and about $7–8 million net tangible operating capital. These are rounded entry-scale references, not an audited January 3 trailing-year statement. Source accounts use different periods and rounding; exact pre-purchase reconciliation is not available in contemporaneous sources recovered here. In particular, a full-year table for the year just beginning is not substituted for information available today. The figures imply high operating returns on tangible capital, subject to verifying the denominator, normal working capital and the amount of excess cash transferred with the business.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: boxed chocolates are purchased both for consumption and as gifts. The purchaser's concern with the recipient's reaction can make dependable quality and an established local name important. That does not eliminate substitution by other sweets or gifts. The retailer must carry inventory, staff shops and maintain product freshness even when demand is concentrated in holidays. Profits depend on realized price per pound after ingredients, labor, occupancy and selling costs. A premium brand has value only if the realized premium exceeds the extra cost of sustaining quality. Regional recognition cannot be assumed to transfer automatically to a new city. These are business-model implications, not an assumption of national expansion or a promised price increase.

Measurement notes: amounts in this reconstruction are nominal dollars, not inflation-adjusted dollars. A historical balance-sheet observation is not a current market quotation. Reported profit, distributable cash and return on tangible operating capital are different quantities. Where a component is missing, owner earnings cannot be calculated precisely by relabeling net income as free cash flow.

The useful reconciliation begins with recurring earnings, adds genuinely noncash charges, and subtracts the expenditures and working capital needed to maintain competitive position. Interest and taxes must be treated consistently with the security being valued. A pretax business yield must not be compared directly with an after-tax common-stock yield. Minority interests, debt, preference claims and cash needed for operations can all change what belongs to the common shareholder. Missing line items are not set to zero. No complete balance sheet, earnings bridge or leverage ratio is implied by the selected figures reproduced here.
