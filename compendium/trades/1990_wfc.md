## Situation before the trade (point-in-time)
**1990 accumulation, not first entry:** California real-estate credit fears made bank assets suspect. The letter describes unusually severe market pessimism about Wells, but the detailed stress test is a **1991 explanation of 1990 decisions**, not a contemporaneous public investment memorandum. Exact first purchase month and entry-date valuation remain unknown. [1]

**Dated explanation, not an entry-date financial filing:** the 1990 letter describes $56 billion of assets, returns exceeding 20% on equity and 1.25% on assets, and an almost 50% share-price decline within months during 1990. Buffett says the purchased interest cost less than five times after-tax earnings and less than three times pretax earnings. Those reported multiples are the recovered valuation context; a trade-by-trade contemporaneous model remains unknown. [1]

## Buffett's thesis in his own words
“We have no interest in purchasing shares of a poorly-managed bank at a ‘cheap’ price.” His alternative was well-managed banks at fair prices. He called Carl Reichardt and Paul Hazen “the best managers in the business.” **Following-year explanation.** [1]

## What Berkshire Hathaway did
About one-sixth of the position was acquired in 1989 and the balance in 1990. At December 1990 it owned 5 million shares costing $289.431 million, market value $289.375 million, just under the ownership threshold then requiring Federal Reserve approval. These are historical share units, not split-adjusted modern shares. [1]

Buffett's stress arithmetic was concrete: over $1 billion annual pretax earnings **after** more than $300 million loan-loss expense; if 10% of all $48 billion loans became troubled in 1991 and lost 30% of principal including foregone interest, Wells would roughly break even. He described that severe case as a low-probability possibility, not a forecast. The point was capacity to absorb losses and recover earning power, not denying credit risk. [1]

## What happened after
The year-end mark was essentially equal to cost; it is not a lifetime outcome. Subsequent adds, regulatory permissions and eventual exit require later evidence; `open` here describes the historical checkpoint, not a claim of ownership in 2026. [1]

## Reasoning drivers checklist
- **Managers:** Reichardt/Hazen's lending and operating discipline mattered because bank leverage magnifies errors. [1]
- **Costs:** Attack expenses even in good years; avoid managerial empire-building. [1]
- **Loss absorption:** Assess whether earning power could withstand severe credit losses rather than predicting a benign California economy. [1]

## Why this trade fits / breaks his pattern
Research interpretation: a leveraged financial franchise can qualify, but low price alone is insufficient. Train on management and loss-bearing capacity, not an indiscriminate “buy banks in recessions” rule. [1]

## Public discussion
The letter records the prevailing California-loss concern; independently authenticated contemporary press and forum discussions remain gaps. No modern social-media reaction is presented as 1990 sentiment. [1]
