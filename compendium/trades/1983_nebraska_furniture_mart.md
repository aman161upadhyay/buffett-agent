## Situation before the trade (point-in-time)
**1983:** Rose Blumkin and her family operated an unusually dominant single-location home-furnishings retailer in Omaha. The following March's letter describes over $100 million annual sales from 200,000 square feet and sales exceeding all local competitors combined. These are disclosure-date observations, not a recovered acquisition-day financial pack. Exact closing day and original earnings multiple remain unknown. [1]

## Buffett's thesis in his own words
“I'd rather wrestle grizzlies than compete with Mrs. B and her progeny.” **Post-purchase explanation**, March 14, 1984. [1]

“It's the ideal business - one built upon exceptional value to the customer that in turn translates into exceptional economics for its owners.” Same letter; shared scale economies, not simply high retail markups. [1]

## What Berkshire Hathaway did
Bought 90% during 1983 for $55 million cash, leaving family managers with 10% and optioning an additional 10% to key young managers. Buffett later said Berkshire did not request an audit, count inventory, verify receivables or check property titles: it relied on the known operator. Do not generalize this highly personal trust decision into a rule to skip due diligence with strangers. [1–2]

## What happened after
The 1989 letter reports company pretax earnings of $17.070 million and continued growth. It also describes Mrs. B's departure in May 1989 over carpet-department disagreements and her new competing business. Buffett sided with the other family managers on that particular operational dispute. These complications prevent depicting the family-management arrangement as frictionless. Exact lifetime return is unknown here. [3]

## Reasoning drivers checklist
- **Cost advantage:** exceptional buying and expense control made competition unattractive. [1]
- **Customer value:** lower costs flowed into lower prices and stronger volume. [1,3]
- **Integrity:** Buffett's stated basis for the unusually simple transaction process. [2]
- **Continuity:** several generations supplied both operating skill and retained ownership. [1]

## Why this trade fits / breaks his pattern
Research interpretation: an excellent private business with self-reinforcing low costs and owner-operators. `open` denotes retained ownership in the cited follow-up, not a new 2026 status audit. [1–3]

## Public discussion
The letters recount earlier Fair Trade litigation and supplier pressure as background to the low-price model, not contemporaneous 1983 investment objections. Original acquisition reporting remains unrecovered. The 1989 disagreement is explicitly disclosed contrary evidence. [1,3]
