Source: https://theoraclesclassroom.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/Coca-Cola-1987.pdf, operating review. Global share is the company's stated estimate with geographic exclusions, not an independently audited US market share. The report also states the fountain business outsold its nearest rival by more than two to one, added 14,000 net outlet accounts and captured nearly three-fourths of that segment's growth over four years.

These figures quantify distribution and market-position advantages without asserting that physical bottling has the same economics as concentrate manufacture. Low parent-company capital needs must be assessed alongside the independent bottlers' capital requirements. International operating-income growth also benefited from a stated 16% strengthening of key currencies against the dollar, so dollar growth was not solely pricing or unit demand.

A matched three-company valuation panel remains unverified after this report, the July 15 interim article and Lu chapter 14 (https://books.google.com/books?id=Lnu0CwAAQBAJ). Operating share and volume comparisons are not mislabeled as peer stock multiples; later market-share or stock-return outcomes are omitted.

## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

Pepsi and other branded beverages compete for consumption, shelf space and restaurant distribution. A strong concentrate franchise may require much less incremental tangible capital than a manufacturer or bottler, so comparing consolidated multiples without checking the asset mix is hazardous. Water and other drinks are also customer alternatives. No dated market-share table, competitor valuation or global consumption table has been recovered in the present source set. The relevant qualitative comparison is the persistence of consumer demand and the economics of the distribution network. A global addressable market is not automatically profitable growth if the producer must subsidize distribution or surrender price to establish a local position.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: a concentrate producer sells a small part of the finished beverage's physical volume but supplies a large part of its consumer identity. Bottlers and retailers supply production, packaging and access to the customer. The division of capital and margin among these participants matters when judging returns. A strong brand can support repeat demand, but advertising, product quality and route-to-market access still require resources. Growth abroad may use a local partner's capital, yet the producer remains exposed to execution, currency and regulation. These are implications of the concentrate-and-bottler model, not an assertion that every incremental dollar of revenue becomes owner cash or that consumption can grow without limit.

Comparison discipline: differences in accounting, capital intensity and financial leverage can make two nominally similar earnings multiples incomparable. A high return on common equity can come from a strong operating franchise or merely from a thin equity base. The comparison that matters is the durable cash return on all necessary capital, followed by the division of that return among the different claimants.

An outside shareholder cannot assume control benefits or private acquisition synergies. A private buyer's price is useful evidence only if the assets, liabilities and ownership rights are comparable. The alternatives also include retaining liquid funds rather than purchasing a mediocre security merely because it is cheaper than an expensive peer. No industry-average multiple or precise competitor ranking is invented here. The qualitative comparisons identify which operating variables need examination; the absence of a recovered comparable-company table is a genuine limit on numerical relative valuation.
