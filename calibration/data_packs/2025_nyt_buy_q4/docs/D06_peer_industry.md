Title: A subscription franchise, not a generic newspaper rebound
Source: https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/71691/000007169125000117/pressrelease06302025.htm ; https://s23.q4cdn.com/152113917/files/doc_events/2025/Apr/30/The-New-York-Times-Company-2024-Annual-Report.pdf
Date: 2025-09-30

The useful comparisons are between direct subscriber franchises, free advertising-funded publishers and entertainment bundles. The Times's ability to increase both digital subscribers and ARPU is more relevant than aggregate newspaper circulation. Conversely, journalism and product development must be renewed continually, and free information competes for attention. Peer names or legacy print multiples alone cannot resolve the company's economics. [1,2]

The reported 6.02 million bundle/multiproduct subscribers are evidence of breadth, not a measured cross-product retention moat. The Athletic's improving adjusted profit is a positive observation, while core news-only subscription revenues declined as mix changed. Digital advertising growth diversifies revenue but introduces marketer cyclicality. No contemporaneous numerical peer-valuation table, churn cohort or independently audited lifetime-value model is recovered. The appropriate industry test asks whether unique reporting, habit and complementary products sustain direct payment after the costs of content, acquisition and platform distribution. It does not assume that every paid media company has the same moat. [1]

Evidence ledger: annual revenue was $2.308321 billion in 2022, $2.426152 billion in 2023 and $2.585919 billion in 2024. Second-quarter revenue of $685.9 million grew 9.7%; operating profit was $106.6 million, up 34.2%, and adjusted operating profit $133.8 million. Diluted EPS was $0.50, or $0.58 adjusted. Total subscribers were 11.88 million, digital-only 11.30 million, and bundle/multiproduct 6.02 million. Digital-only ARPU was $9.64, up 3.2%. Digital subscription revenue was $350.4 million, total subscription revenue $481.4 million, digital advertising $94.4 million and total advertising $134.0 million. Affiliate/licensing/other revenue was $70.5 million. Marketing costs were $69.2 million; journalism and subscriber-servicing spending remain real operating costs. [1,2]

Comparison discipline: industry membership does not make two businesses economically identical. Geography, customer mix, contract duration, capital intensity, leverage and accounting adjustments can dominate headline growth comparisons. No synchronized peer-price and consensus-earnings dataset was recovered for this cutoff; relative cheapness is therefore not asserted numerically. The useful comparison is between sources of durable return and exposure to common pressures. A competitor's weakness is not automatically this company's moat, and an industry recovery is not required to be uniform across participants.
