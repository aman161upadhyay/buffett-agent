## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

The September 30 transaction terms call for approximately $420 million in Berkshire stock, specified as 25,221 shares. At the day's quoted $16,675 Berkshire price, that is about $420.56 million, an analyst calculation. This is the announced proposal, not a fixed cash check. Dexter's audited trailing earnings, working-capital needs and maintenance-capital budget have not been recovered. No acquisition earnings multiple is invented. Paying in shares exchanges a continuing fraction of the buyer's operating businesses for the seller's business. That requires valuing both sides. No final issued-share count or retrospective alternative purchase-price estimate is inserted into the proposal as a fact already known.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: footwear requires design, purchasing, manufacturing or sourcing, distribution and inventory across styles and sizes. Unsold inventory can be worth less than recorded cost. A good domestic factory can still be vulnerable if imports deliver an acceptable product at materially lower cost. Brand, quality and delivery speed may support customer loyalty, but each must be tested against the consumer's alternatives. Owning stores can capture retail margin while adding leases, inventory and fixed expenses. Efficient operations are valuable only to the extent that they sustain economic returns after all capital requirements. This is a description of competitive possibilities at the cutoff, not a prediction that domestic production must succeed or fail.

Measurement notes: amounts in this reconstruction are nominal dollars, not inflation-adjusted dollars. A historical balance-sheet observation is not a current market quotation. Reported profit, distributable cash and return on tangible operating capital are different quantities. Where a component is missing, owner earnings cannot be calculated precisely by relabeling net income as free cash flow.

The useful reconciliation begins with recurring earnings, adds genuinely noncash charges, and subtracts the expenditures and working capital needed to maintain competitive position. Interest and taxes must be treated consistently with the security being valued. A pretax business yield must not be compared directly with an after-tax common-stock yield. Minority interests, debt, preference claims and cash needed for operations can all change what belongs to the common shareholder. Missing line items are not set to zero. No complete balance sheet, earnings bridge or leverage ratio is implied by the selected figures reproduced here.
