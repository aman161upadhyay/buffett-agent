## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

The proposed all-stock transaction is valued at about $22 billion at announcement prices. General Re adds approximately $24 billion of invested assets in period deal descriptions. Each General Re share is to receive 0.0035 Berkshire Class A shares or 0.105 Class B shares. The stock ratios, rather than an invariant dollar amount, define the economic exchange. A fully reconciled latest loss-reserve schedule, capital requirement and look-through minority adjustment were not recovered in the pack. The invested assets cannot simply be subtracted from the price as surplus cash because policyholder and reinsurance obligations support much of that portfolio. Financial-products exposures require their own capital and counterparty analysis.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: an insurer receives premiums before all associated losses are known or paid. The investment balance generated by that delay is economically valuable only when the eventual underwriting cost is acceptable. Inadequate prices or underestimated reserves can create reported profits that do not exist economically. A direct seller can avoid agency commissions, but saving distribution costs does not compensate for accepting unsuitable risks at inadequate rates. Regulatory capital and claims-paying ability constrain the amount that can be invested in equities or distributed to owners. Reinsurance adds counterparty exposure and can transfer risk without eliminating uncertainty about its measurement. This is a description of insurance mechanics, not a claim that any reported reserve balance is sufficient.

Measurement notes: amounts in this reconstruction are nominal dollars, not inflation-adjusted dollars. A historical balance-sheet observation is not a current market quotation. Reported profit, distributable cash and return on tangible operating capital are different quantities. Where a component is missing, owner earnings cannot be calculated precisely by relabeling net income as free cash flow.

The useful reconciliation begins with recurring earnings, adds genuinely noncash charges, and subtracts the expenditures and working capital needed to maintain competitive position. Interest and taxes must be treated consistently with the security being valued. A pretax business yield must not be compared directly with an after-tax common-stock yield. Minority interests, debt, preference claims and cash needed for operations can all change what belongs to the common shareholder. Missing line items are not set to zero. No complete balance sheet, earnings bridge or leverage ratio is implied by the selected figures reproduced here.
