## Latest verified release

The annual report dated March 17, 1973 is the latest verified operating release here, covering December 31, 1972. Revenue $217.844m versus $192.749m rose 13.0%; operating income $21.806m versus $15.194m rose 43.5%; comparable earnings before extraordinary items $10.015m versus $6.784m rose 47.6%; corresponding EPS $2.08 versus $1.52 rose 36.8%. The EPS/earnings growth difference reflects the share base. Operating margin increased from 7.9% to 10.0%. January 1973's announced quarterly dividend increased from $.05 to $.10, a $.40 annualized rate, not a dividend already paid for a full year.

Management expected healthy overall profit growth in 1973, without numerical guidance, while warning that newspaper costs would make the prior productivity/profitability increase difficult to repeat. Every 1972 quarter had greater revenue and profit than its year-earlier counterpart, but no numerical Q1 1973 release was recovered. Searches of the 1972 report, the 1971 corporate archive (https://www.ghco.com/static-files/20476aba-bd06-4a64-91a6-db13e21b7137) and Lu chapter 8 (https://books.google.com/books?id=Lnu0CwAAQBAJ) did not establish a pre-May 1 quarterly publication. This annual comparison is a substitute, not a fabricated latest quarter.

## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

The latest complete annual record shows revenue up about 13% and operating income up about 43.5%, calculated from $217.844/$192.749 million and $21.806/$15.194 million. The annual letter says each division's operating income improved: newspapers 17%, magazine/books 111% and broadcasting 56%. It also warns of rising materials, services and labor costs at the Post. In January the board doubles the regular quarterly dividend from $0.05 to $0.10. No first-quarter earnings release is recovered. Management's annual outlook for growth is a forecast, not a demonstrated current result; the desk should preserve that distinction rather than carry the 1972 growth rates forward mechanically.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: newspapers and broadcasters sell audiences to advertisers while supplying news and entertainment to readers or viewers. An established local position can be difficult to replicate because a new entrant must build both audience and advertising support. Broadcast licenses add regulatory obligations and are not equivalent to perpetual unregulated monopolies. Advertising is sensitive to business conditions; labor, programming and newsprint costs need not decline when advertising weakens. The distinction between a valuable local franchise and the price paid for it is essential. Scale across properties can help administration, but no unverified synergy is counted in the economics presented here. This paragraph describes the competitive mechanism rather than assigning a private-market valuation to any individual property.

Reporting discipline: a full fiscal year's results are not available merely because part of that year has elapsed. The observations above are restricted to the reporting periods identified, and no later quarter is backfilled. An annualized partial period would be a calculation, not a reported result; no such annualization is used unless expressly identified.

For decision purposes, the key question is whether the recent change alters recurring earning power or only its timing. Volume, realized price, operating expenses and capital requirements should be considered separately. A price increase that merely offsets higher input costs is not necessarily an improvement in economics. Similarly, a recovery in accounting profit is not proof that competitive advantage has returned. Where a current operating statistic has not been recovered, this document makes no assertion about its direction. The evidence permits a conditional assessment of durability, not a precise estimate of an unavailable earnings announcement.
