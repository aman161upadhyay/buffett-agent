These dated pre-crisis multiples document dispersion, not a matched-date October bargain list. Buying an index combines businesses with different capital requirements and claims on funding. Energy profit can be commodity-driven, software margins depend on competitive position, and bank profit depends on credit marks and leverage. A market-level earnings yield does not make those risks disappear through averaging. The target mandate is a portfolio stance, so these are constituent alternatives rather than peers to a single issuer.

The contemporaneous October 3 employment release in D03 reports September job losses of 159,000, including 35,000 construction and 51,000 manufacturing jobs. The 67.61 October 16 VIX reading records acute option-implied volatility, not a fundamental earnings forecast or a probability of permanent loss. Treasury yield of 3.99% and a historical dividend run-rate sensitivity near 3.05% describe different claims; neither determines the outcome of a subsequent recession.

The alternatives are short government obligations, long nominal bonds, strong operating businesses and weaker enterprises dependent on refinancing. Evaluate balance-sheet strength before comparing earnings yields. Cash has liquidity value in a funding crisis; long-duration nominal bonds have inflation and reinvestment risks. The analysis is about relative investment terms, not a binary claim that equities are always superior to government securities. [Source above.]

Technical context: cash, bills, long bonds and productive assets serve different purposes. Short government instruments preserve nominal liquidity but expose holders to reinvestment rates and purchasing-power erosion. A long bond fixes nominal payments while adding duration risk. A public equity is a residual ownership claim whose value depends on sustainable distributable cash, not tomorrow's quote. A controlled operating business adds capital-allocation rights and illiquidity. Compare after-tax expected economics on consistent horizons, retaining enough liquidity for obligations before allocating discretionary capital. The size of a cash balance alone does not identify a market forecast or explain every trade. [Source above; comparative investment framework, not a new macroeconomic estimate.]

Comparison discipline: compare businesses on the source of their cash and the capital required to earn it, rather than treating the nearest ticker as an exact substitute. Normalize financing and accounting differences before interpreting relative multiples. A peer can have a better balance sheet but a worse franchise, or a better franchise at an unattractive price. Relative cheapness alone cannot prove absolute value. Consider the opportunity cost of adding to an existing understandable holding, retaining liquidity, owning an entire business or buying the investor's own shares. Where a numerical peer table was not recovered, keep the comparison qualitative rather than inventing an industry average.

Evidence boundary: missing figures are not available in contemporaneous sources recovered for this digest. Analytical questions are not additional reported facts or claims about the investor's private thinking. Read the financial, risk and fund-context documents together; no individual accounting ratio supplies a complete investment conclusion.
