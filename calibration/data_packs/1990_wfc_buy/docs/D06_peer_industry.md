## Cutoff evidence — reconstructed

The July 18 report contrasts Wells Fargo with Citicorp, whose second-quarter net income falls to $248 million from $395 million. Citicorp attributes weakness to real estate and unpaid interest from Argentina and Brazil; it reports $92 million net commercial-loan writeoffs, including $42 million from US real estate, against $29 million total a year earlier. These are period peer warnings, not losses assigned to Wells. Wells's own profit growth includes the Nikko gain, so the raw growth rates are not directly comparable. A sound comparison needs funding, recurring expenses, provisions and credit exposure; a low multiple alone does not distinguish an efficient bank from a poor one.

## Economic reading of the supplied facts

Industry economics: a bank's assets consist largely of promises to repay. Small changes in the value of loans can have large effects on a leveraged common-equity base. Deposits provide funding, but do not make credit losses disappear. A bank's expense discipline matters because pre-provision earnings can absorb losses before capital is impaired. Commercial-property lending requires attention to borrower equity, collateral and refinancing rather than merely the last reported interest payment. Reported earnings can be overstated when loss provisions lag actual deterioration. Comparing banks therefore requires both operating efficiency and a conservative estimate of asset quality. These mechanics explain why a low price-to-earnings ratio alone is not sufficient evidence of a bargain, and why depositor funding is not a substitute for adequate capital.

Comparison discipline: differences in accounting, capital intensity and financial leverage can make two nominally similar earnings multiples incomparable. A high return on common equity can come from a strong operating franchise or merely from a thin equity base. The comparison that matters is the durable cash return on all necessary capital, followed by the division of that return among the different claimants.

An outside shareholder cannot assume control benefits or private acquisition synergies. A private buyer's price is useful evidence only if the assets, liabilities and ownership rights are comparable. The alternatives also include retaining liquid funds rather than purchasing a mediocre security merely because it is cheaper than an expensive peer. No industry-average multiple or precise competitor ranking is invented here. The qualitative comparisons identify which operating variables need examination; the absence of a recovered comparable-company table is a genuine limit on numerical relative valuation.
